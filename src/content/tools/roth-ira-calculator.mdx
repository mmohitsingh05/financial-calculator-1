  - question: What's the difference between a Roth and Traditional IRA?
    answer: The key difference is when you pay taxes. Roth IRA contributions are made with after-tax dollars — you get no upfront tax deduction, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free. Traditional IRA contributions may be tax-deductible now, but withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. Roth IRAs also have no required minimum distributions (RMDs), making them excellent for estate planning. For young investors in lower tax brackets, Roth IRAs are particularly attractive.
  - question: What are the income limits for Roth IRA contributions?
    answer: In 2026, you can contribute the full amount to a Roth IRA if your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) is under $150,000 (single) or $240,000 (married filing jointly). Contributions phase out between $150,000–$165,000 (single) and $240,000–$255,000 (married). Above those limits, you cannot contribute directly to a Roth IRA, but you may be able to use a "backdoor Roth IRA" strategy — contributing to a Traditional IRA first, then converting it to Roth.

A **Roth IRA calculator** shows you the remarkable power of tax-free growth. Unlike Traditional IRAs that give you a tax break now but tax withdrawals later, Roth IRAs offer the opposite — you contribute after-tax dollars, but your money grows tax-free and you pay zero taxes on qualified withdrawals in retirement. For young investors with decades of growth ahead, this tax-free compounding is incredibly valuable.

## How to Use This Calculator

Enter your current age, current Roth IRA balance, your planned annual contribution (up to the annual limit), and your expected annual return rate. Enter your planned retirement age to see your projected balance. The calculator shows the total contributions you've made, the amount those contributions grew to, and — critically — the fact that all of the growth is tax-free when you withdraw it in retirement.

## How It's Calculated

The same future value formula used for other investments applies:

**FV = P × (1 + r/n)^(n×t) + PMT × [ ((1 + r/n)^(n×t) − 1) / (r/n) ]**

The difference is in the tax treatment, not the math. Roth IRA contributions are made after tax, so there's no tax deduction now. However, qualified withdrawals (after age 59½ and at least 5 years after the first contribution) are entirely tax-free — including all the growth. The calculator applies annual contribution limits and MAGI phase-out ranges as configurable inputs.
